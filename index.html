How bioactive discovery works

How bioactive discovery works — the field’s method, not BioActive’s. Before anyone talks about a product, the field runs a sequence. It starts with observation, moves to mechanism, and only then to a trial — and a trial is graded on what it actually showed.

Step 1

Observe

Diets rich in polyphenols — plant compounds found in fruits, vegetables, tea, and cocoa — are associated in cohort and case-control studies with lower risk of chronic disease. The review that summarises this work is careful about what it is: a narrative review of hypothesized protective effects, drawing mainly on animal models, feeding experiments, and human cohort and case-control studies (Cory et al., 2018).

Step 2

Connect to mechanism

The gut microbiota links what we eat to immune, metabolic, and neurobehavioural traits. Diet and fibre can shift the microbiota, and greater microbiome diversity and high-fibre intake are related to lower long-term weight gain. Targeting the microbiome with probiotics or dietary fibre benefits health and could potentially reduce obesity — “could potentially” is the authors’ own wording (Valdes et al., 2018).

Step 3

Test at a fixed dose

A randomized trial tests one compound, at one dose, in one population. A large randomized trial of a bioactive — COSMOS — gave 21,442 older adults 500 mg of cocoa flavanols a day (including 80 mg of epicatechin) or a placebo for a median of 3.6 years (Sesso et al., 2022).

Step 4

Grade what it showed

COSMOS did not significantly reduce total cardiovascular events — its primary endpoint (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.78–1.02). Cardiovascular death, a secondary endpoint, was 27% lower (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.54–0.98). The authors’ own conclusion: additional research is warranted. A single trial, however large, is one rung — not the top of the ladder (Sesso et al., 2022).

The field

Three published results anchor this page

The field — independent field research, not BioActive work. None of them is a BioActive result, and none of them is a product benchmark. Each is cited in full.

One large trial of a bioactive — Sesso et al. (COSMOS), 2022. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of a cocoa extract supplement — 500 mg of flavanols a day, including 80 mg of (−)-epicatechin — against placebo in 21,442 older adults (women aged 65 and over, men aged 60 and over), free of major cardiovascular disease and recently diagnosed cancer, followed for a median of 3.6 years. Total cardiovascular events, the primary outcome, were 10% lower with cocoa extract but the result did not reach statistical significance (410 events vs 456; HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.78–1.02; P = 0.11). Cardiovascular death, a secondary endpoint, was 27% lower (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.54–0.98). The authors conclude: “Cocoa extract supplementation did not significantly reduce total cardiovascular events among older adults but reduced CVD death by 27%… Additional research is warranted.”

Sesso et al., “Effect of cocoa flavanol supplementation for the prevention of cardiovascular disease events: the COcoa Supplement and Multivitamin Outcomes Study (COSMOS) randomized clinical trial,” The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition 115(6), 1490–1500 (2022), DOI 10.1093/ajcn/nqac055. Trial registration NCT02422745.

What polyphenols are hypothesized to do — Cory et al., 2018. A narrative mini-review of polyphenols — plant compounds — and their role in health outcomes and non-communicable diseases. The publications it draws on come mainly from animal models and feeding experiments, as well as human cohort and case-control studies. It evaluates hypothesized protective effects of polyphenols in obesity, neurodegenerative diseases, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases. Hypothesized is the review’s own word: this is a summary of what has been proposed and where the evidence stands, not a finding that polyphenols prevent disease.

Cory, Passarelli, Szeto, Tamez & Mattei, “The Role of Polyphenols in Human Health and Food Systems: A Mini-Review,” Frontiers in Nutrition 5, 87 (2018), DOI 10.3389/fnut.2018.00087.

Diet, the microbiome, and health — Valdes et al., 2018. A review of how the gut microbiota connects diet to health. Gut microbes are key to many aspects of human health, including immune, metabolic, and neurobehavioural traits. Gut microbiome diversity and high-fibre intake are related to lower long-term weight gain. The authors’ key message: “Targeting the gut microbiome, with probiotics or dietary fibre, benefits human health and could potentially reduce obesity.” Could potentially is their wording — an association and a plausible route, not a demonstrated outcome.

Valdes, Walter, Segal & Spector, “Role of the gut microbiota in nutrition and health,” BMJ 361, k2179 (2018), DOI 10.1136/bmj.k2179.

What this section does not show: no item above is a BioActive result, a product benchmark, or a claim any product could make. None of the three sources supports the wellness phrases “supports gut-barrier integrity,” “supports healthy glucose metabolism,” or “supports skin barrier function” — and none of those phrases appears anywhere else on this page. COSMOS tested one compound at one dose in one population; its primary endpoint missed significance, and the 27% figure is a secondary endpoint. Cory et al. is a review of hypothesized effects, largely from animal and observational work. Valdes et al. is a review of largely associational evidence.
Where bioactives are studied

Three areas, from the cited work

Cardiovascular health

A cocoa flavanol extract tested in a large randomized trial — 21,442 older adults, 500 mg a day, median 3.6 years. The primary endpoint missed significance; cardiovascular death, a secondary endpoint, was 27% lower (Sesso et al., 2022).

Polyphenols and chronic disease

Hypothesized protective effects of plant polyphenols evaluated across obesity, neurodegenerative diseases, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases — on evidence drawn mainly from animal models, feeding experiments, and human cohort and case-control studies (Cory et al., 2018).

Gut microbiota and diet

Diet and fibre modulate the gut microbiota; greater microbiome diversity and high-fibre intake are related to lower long-term weight gain, and targeting the microbiome with probiotics or dietary fibre could potentially benefit health (Valdes et al., 2018).

What is not covered here: skin, sleep, cognition, and immune/respiratory areas are not demonstrated by the work cited on this page, and they do not appear as studied areas above.

Mission · Vision · Governance

What the idea holds itself to

Mission

Make the health potential of natural compounds discoverable, verifiable, and accessible.

Vision

A field where the evidence for a natural compound is as visible as any product made from it.

Governance — the principles this concept holds itself to

  • Scientific integrity: Separate AI predictions from validated evidence; never present a prediction as proof.
  • Transparency: Publish evidence summaries, limitations, and study methods.
  • Ethical sourcing: Use traceable botanical supply chains, fair benefit-sharing, and respect for Indigenous knowledge.
  • Privacy: Minimize consumer-data collection, anonymize outcome data, and give users control over sharing.
  • Safety first: Monitor adverse events, interactions, contaminants, and vulnerable populations.
  • Regulatory alignment: Adapt claims, product classification, and market entry by jurisdiction.

These are principles, not claims about current work.
